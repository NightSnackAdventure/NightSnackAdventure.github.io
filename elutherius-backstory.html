Those Who Shaped Him

The People Who Made Elutherius Aurum

Regina Aurum

Mother

Regina means queen, and she has always known it. Her authority is not conditional—she does not become queen through achievement or lose it through failure. She simply is, in the way that fundamental facts are true regardless of circumstance.

She gave Therius the impulsiveness. The restlessness, the inability to leave well enough alone, the need for motion that makes sitting still feel like suffocation. In her, these qualities are contained—not eliminated, but channeled into purposeful action. She understands exactly why he is the way he is, which does not mean she knows what to do about it. Understanding and fixing are different problems, and she has learned the difference.

She can be terrifying. The kind of presence that changes the temperature of a room when she enters it, that makes administrators reconsider their positions and faculty members choose their words more carefully. She has a temper that runs hot and burns clean, leaving no lingering resentment once it passes.

She is also capable of tremendous warmth. The kind of mother who will stay up late helping with research, who remembers preferences and particular anxieties, who loves in practical ways that solve problems before they become crises. When Therius was young, she would intercept his father's disappointment with her body, taking the brunt of Lawrence's lectures herself and then privately delivering her own version—sharper, in some ways more cutting, but followed by reassurance that he was still loved.

After the incident, she made calls. Quietly, efficiently, without asking permission. She contacted a relative on her husband's side—the one in possession of a specific family heirloom: the Staff of Charming. The staff had never been on the table for Elutherius, the black seep of the family. But Regina Aurum wasn't asking. She presented it to Therius wrapped in cloth, saying only "for your research" with complete sincerity and no further comment. She did not mention the incident. She did not need to.

She still writes to him. Every week, without fail, he receives a carefully crafted letter from her. It pains him to read them, but it would pain him more not to. He only responds every couple of months, briefly, just to let her know he's still alive and working on his research. He carries guilt for not writing more often. But, in his own words, "Dont you think I've already hurt her enough?"

Lawrence Aurum

Father

Lawrence means crowned with laurel—victorious, celebrated, recognized for achievement. The laurel crown is not inherent. It must be earned through victory, and once earned, it begins immediately to wither. This is the principle by which Lawrence Aurum has organized his entire life, and it has made him smaller than he used to be.

Lawrence Aurum offers approval conditionally. When his son Marcus publishes, Lawrence is proud—vocally, publicly proud in the way that costs him nothing because it reflects well on the family. When Olivia, her youngest, chose architecture, he was quietly relieved—she found a different arena, no longer a variable in the calculation of the family's academic standing. When Therius causes problems, Lawrence experiences it as personal failure. Every incident is evidence that something in the family mechanism is broken, and broken things threaten the stability of everything.

He does not mean to be cruel. He genuinely believes he is helping. Consequences build character. High standards produce excellence. These are principles he has organized his life around, and they have worked for him, so they should work for his sons. That they have not worked for Therius is not a flaw in the principles but in Therius, and if Therius would simply try harder, apply himself more seriously, care about his reputation the way Marcus does—

Therius has not been in contact with his father after he left, neither has his mother asked him to. He believes his father is content with this arrangement.

Marcus Aurum

Older Brother • The One Who Made It Look Easy

Marcus, Therius's older brother, is a conjuration specialist. He's at least as brilliant as him, without the uncontrollable chaos that follows Therius. Marcus has his mother's fire, but in him it manifests as charming spontaneity, rather than the destructive force of his younger brother. He's creative. Eloquent. Whimsical, in a controlled, tasteful way. He was everything Therius couldn't be. Still is, in many ways.

As children, they were close. Fought constantly in the way siblings do, but also played together, caused trouble together, developed the kind of shorthand that comes from growing up in the same house with the same parents. Marcus was the one who taught Therius the minor illusion cantrip that got them both grounded for a week. Therius was the one who convinced Marcus to sneak into their father's study to look at the restricted texts, which they both now agree was a poor decision but also genuinely educational.

The distance started in their teenage years, when academic performance began to matter in ways it had not before. Marcus was brilliant—genuinely, effortlessly brilliant in the way that makes things look easy even when they are not. He also had Regina's charm without Therius's chaos. He could be spontaneous in ways that seemed whimsical rather than disruptive, could take risks that came across as bold rather than reckless. People liked him. Professors liked him. Everything he did seemed to confirm that he belonged.

Therius was measured against this. "Why can't you be more like Marcus?" The words were only said out loud a few times, but that was enough to leave a lasting impression. The shadow of comparison was there in every conversation, every assessment, every time Marcus succeeded at something Therius had struggled with. And Therius could not help but resent it, even though Marcus had done nothing to deserve it.

Marcus, meanwhile, at some point made a choice—probably unconscious, probably gradual—to care about his reputation at school. To protect it. And protecting it meant creating distance from the brother who kept getting into situations that required explanation. Not cutting contact, nothing so dramatic. Just becoming polite where he had been close. Friendly where he had been intimate. The kind of brother you have dinner with at family events but do not call for when something goes wrong.

The relationship is carefully managed, bounded by conventions that keep it comfortable and prevent either of them from addressing the fact that there is a rift, although they both know it exists, and they are both choosing not to repair it. When they see each other now, they ask about research and make small talk and avoid anything that might require actual vulnerability.

Olivia Aurum

Younger Sister

Olivia was the charming one. Still is, probably. The youngest child who learned early how to deploy cuteness strategically, how to get away with things through sheer force of personality, how to make adults smile even when she had just done something technically against the rules. She was clever in ways that felt delightful rather than threatening. When she asked questions, people wanted to answer them. When she broke things, people wanted to help her fix them.

Her talent turned out to be architecture. Not magic—or not primarily magic. Their parents were delighted. Lawrence, because her success reflected well on the family and required no comparison to his own work. Regina, because Olivia had found something she loved that would not consume her the way magic can consume practitioners. Both of them, because she had chosen a path that was impressive without being dangerous, that would give her stability and respect without the chaos that seems to follow wizards who push boundaries.

When they were young—before Marcus pulled away, before Therius started accumulating incidents—Olivia and Therius were co-conspirators. She was the lookout while he picked the lock on their father's study. He was the distraction while she smuggled pastries from the kitchen. They had the kind of partnership that comes from being the two youngest, the two who were not Marcus, the two who had to make their own entertainment while the golden child was off being golden.

Then she grew up. Not all at once, but gradually, in the way that happens around twelve or thirteen when children start noticing social dynamics and caring about consequences. She began to understand that while her chaos was charming, his was becoming genuinely concerning. That the incidents he caused had weight, left damage, required cleanup that affected the whole family. She did not stop loving him. She simply stopped being his accomplice.

She has written him a few times. She's told him about her projects. The library she is designing for a small city north of the old capital. The house she is renovating that has structural problems no one noticed until she examined the foundations. She asked about his research, in that careful sideways way that actually wants to know how he is feeling, but is being gracious about it. He didn't tell her about the fighting rings or the potions. He simply summarized his progress on his research, and told her his pillow is uncomfortable.

Meredith Stormcoven

The One He Did Not Love Enough

Meredith came from another old wizarding family. Their families knew each other, moved in the same social circles, attended the same university functions. The relationship was almost inevitable, which should have been a warning but felt instead like confirmation that it was meant to be.

She had strawberry blonde hair and bright blue eyes. She was kind—genuinely kind, even when it cost her something. She would clean up other people's messes when she did not have to. She would listen to problems she had no obligation to solve. She saw good in people even when the evidence suggested she should not, and she held onto that vision with stubborn determination.

She was also volatile and prone to insecurity. Would have moments of doubt, emotional instability that she managed as well as she could. She threw tantrums occasionally—not often, but enough that he learned to recognize the signs—and then apologized profusely afterwards, sometimes overcorrecting into excessive accommodation. She would sulk when hurt or jealous, but eventually offer a hug, testing whether he was still there, still willing to work through it. And he always was, in those moments. It was only later, in the quiet between incidents, that he would wonder whether he was staying because he loved her or because leaving felt cruel.

But in her good moments, she was full of creative energy. She created beautiful illusions—timelapses of flowers blooming, baby unicorns taking their first steps, music boxes that played melodies matching the listener's mood. She wrote in colored ink and drew hearts in the margins of her journals. She had a St. Burmese cat named Diva who tolerated Therius's presence but made clear through consistent cold shoulder that he was not welcome in her hierarchy. The cat always came first. He learned to accept this.

She wanted a life he could picture clearly. A tower with a garden, vines growing up the walls, flowers in colors she had not yet decided on but would spend hours debating. Children—two, probably, though she was open to negotiation. She had names picked out already, written in one of her journals alongside sketches of what the garden might look like. She was not in a rush. But she had a vision, and it included him, and she made space for him in it without demanding he confirm whether he wanted the same things.

He tried. This is important to understand. He tried to want what she wanted, to become the person who could give her the life she deserved. He tried to settle, to imagine himself in the tower with the garden and the children and the permanent stability of a life that had clear shape and purpose. He could picture it. He simply could not make himself want it the way she did.

He sent the message via Magic Mouth before going to meet Saffron. Ended the relationship, used a second-level spell, treated it like official business that required magical documentation. The spell delivered the message to Meredith while she was in her study group. Not alone—she was never alone when she needed to be, and he had not checked, had not thought to confirm—and the dissolution of their relationship became public before she had processed it privately.

He thinks about this more than he should. The fact that she had to hear "we're done" in front of her peers, that she had to manage her reaction with witnesses, that the humiliation was compounded by publicity. He had tried to be clean about it—to end things before anything else happened, to give her the technical truth that he had not physically cheated—but clean and kind are not the same thing, and the method he chose was efficient at the cost of being devastatingly unkind.

She might have the garden now. The tower. The two children with names from her list. Diva is probably still there, older, still ruling her small kingdom with the same dismissive authority. Therius hopes Meredith found someone who wants what she wants, who looks at her vision of the future and sees something they desire rather than something they are trying to convince themselves to accept. She deserved that. She deserved better than him. These facts are both true and do not make him feel less guilty about the way he ended things.

The Crossroads

The Incident he's still running from

First there were only rumors. Then an official warning from faculty. Because of who he is a person, this immediately raised Therius' interest onto an unbearable level. In retrospect, one might argue the cracks in his relationship with Meredith may have contributed to his stubborn desire to investigate.

Naturally, she was bitterly jealous as he brought up his plan. He was not going to do anything weird, he insisted. He just thought he could convince her to hunt elsewhere. And, naturally, he had a few questions only she could answer. It would be purely research. He had planned it thoroughly, studied the ways of Infernal laws, protected himself against charms.

This was not good enough for Meredith. She pleaded with him not to go. When pleading didn't work, she exploded. She didn't kick him out of her apartment. Instead, she left, slammed the door after her, not to return until the next afternoon.

Therius made a decision. One that had been floating in his mind as a possibility for weeks, maybe months. He was not going to betray her, of course. He was sure to avoid such unnecessarily cruelty. He would do everything in the proper order-he left a message, in his own voice, telling her it was over. Then he left her apartment.

He's imagined how the events of the next afternoon went down many times. Meredith would have returned to her apartment with her study group. Immediately as they stepped in, they would have heard Therius' voice, and realized it was coming from the statuette. A marble horse head; Meredith's favorite, which she always kept on the shelf over her bed. In retrospect, he should have probably used a different object.

They would have listened in silence as his voice told her it was over. That he was sorry. Then, they would have heard him hesitate for a moment as he was casting, as he decided he could trust Meredith and she deserved to know the truth. They would have heard the words, "I am going to see the succubus." Straight from the horse's mouth.

Saffron

The Catalyst

Flaming, curly locks of auburn. Ivory skin. Eyes of emerald green. She was physically beautiful and stacked in defiance of the gods, but that was the least dangerous thing about her.

She was not what people imagine when they hear the word succubus. There was no sultry seductress, no creature of pure lust radiating obvious threat. Instead there was warmth. Genuine interest. The sense that she was paying attention—not performing attention, but actually caring about what you say, tracking the details, remembering what matters to you. This quality, cranked to eleven, felt natural-if it wasn't for the sheer intensity of it that, in hindsight, was out of place.

He had done the research. Read the literature on infernal contracts, charm magic, the mechanisms by which succubi operate. He knew the risks. He negotiated terms—explicit, carefully worded protections against direct harm and magical compulsion. He approached the situation as a researcher investigating a phenomenon poorly documented in the primary sources. He was the one making her sign a contract, not the other way around. He felt so clever about it, too.

The terms of the contract were solid. He checked them carefully, made sure there were no obvious loopholes that would give her claim to his soul or his service or any other standard succubus payment. He was especially clear that no charm magic would be used on him. He felt confident to proceed with his plan.

What the literature had not prepared him for was the possibility that the warmth might feel real. That she might laugh at his jokes in ways that seemed genuinely delighted rather than performed. That the conversations might flow with the kind of easy rhythm that usually takes months to develop. The technical term for this is "infernal manipulation of mortal attachment patterns." The experiential term is "it felt like she actually liked him," and the gap between those framings is the source of ongoing distress.

He was, of course, never foolish enough to believe any of it was real. That would have been the interplanar equivalent of thinking a stripper is in love with you. He had only ever been a potential target, nothing more. Never mind the fact that such sophisticated emulation of warmth was unprecedented. Nevermind that his nervous system reacted as if it was real. It was all a mirage.

Whatever happened that night, he never told anyone. He had meant to talk about his findings eventually, of course. But that was all before he found out what had transpired at Meredith's apartment. He returned to the university, and found himself known as the one who'd betrayed his girlfriend for a succubus. A wizard who couldn't resist Infernal temptation. Unreliable, untrustworthy, and a danger to everyone around him. And when both your families are well-known and connected, the world is a small place. It didn't take many days, until there was not a wizard in the city who didn't know about Therius, the Succubus Guy. His peers. Faculty. His own family.

He tried to defend himself. But he soon realized there was no acceptable defense for what he did. He stuck with the explanation that it was premeditated. That he had most definitely not been charmed. He had been in control, executed his plan, gotten what he wanted. This did not actually look any better than having a lapse of judgement. He still fiercely maintains he was not charmed. His mother is the only one who believes him. She was still, rightfully, furious, and then emotionally distraught for a long time.

What he told no one about, not even his mother, was the piece of paper he had signed. He'd tossed it into a lake, in a box along with his notes about the encounter, immediately after he learned about his new public standing. He's gone on with his life, pretending it never existed.

He came to Adanesne for a new beginning, yet his past still haunts him from the inside. He's terrified of it coming out, to tarnish his reputation once more. He takes precautions to prevent anything similar from getting to him again. He has developed a fear of being charmed and losing control, of what that would do to others' perceptions of him. His usual recklessness gives way to caution whenever anything Infernal is involved. And his worst fear-not even that he still owes something, even though he can't bear to think about that-is someone finding out what he left in the bottom of a lake.
