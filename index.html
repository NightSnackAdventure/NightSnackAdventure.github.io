Elutherius Aurum

"Therius"

Evocation Wizard • Scholar of the Ether

TL;DR, spoiler free

  • Post-graduate wizard in his early thirties.
  • Arrived to Adanesne some 3-4ish years ago as an undergraduate.
  • Working on his thesis on the theoretical underpinnings of evocation magic.
  • Tries to maintain an aura of respectability, but is actually thrill-seeking and impulsive.
  • Left his home country for Adanesne after a failed relationship. He is elusive about the exact details.
  • Microdoses potions to stay alert and gambles at the fighting rings.

    • The Paradox of Freedom

    The name itself is a manifesto. Elutherius—from the ancient word for freedom, for liberation from bondage. It was chosen deliberately, perhaps by parents who understood what it meant to live under constraint, or perhaps by the man himself when he realized that names, like magic, are declarations of intent. Either way, it suits him. The Ether recognizes no masters, bends to no divine will, answers only to those who understand its fundamental nature as pure, unbounded force. So too does Elutherius Aurum approach the world: with the conviction that freedom is not merely a right but a cosmic principle, woven into the very fabric of reality.

    Yet freedom, he has discovered, is not simple. It is not the absence of structure but the negotiation between what one wishes to be and what one must appear to be. Between the demands of scholarship—with its hierarchies and conventions and unspoken rules about proper conduct—and the restless need for motion, for risk, for the electric thrill of uncertainty. Most days, he feels suspended between these competing aspects of himself, never quite at peace with either, always conscious of performing one while suppressing the other.

    The Tension Within

    To understand Therius is to understand that he exists in a state of constant negotiation with himself. On one hand, there is the scholar — methodical, ambitious, genuinely fascinated by the theoretical frameworks that govern magical force. This is the man who has spent countless hours in Erytreum's libraries, who takes meticulous notes on everything from combat applications to the comparative analysis of elemental summoning, who dreams of tenure and recognition and perhaps, one day, a tower of his own where he might conduct research undisturbed.

    On the other hand, there is something wilder in him. A need for immediacy, for sensation, for experiences that cannot be captured in footnotes and citations. This is the man who frequents the fighting rings — both the mortal combat arenas and the more esoteric summon battles — not merely to observe but to feel the rush of uncertainty, the spike of adrenaline when real stakes hang in the balance. When he places bets, when he sends his bonded fire elemental Sparky into the ring, when he stands perhaps a bit too close to the edge of a battlefield because he has True Strike and believes himself invincible — these are not acts of pure recklessness. They are expressions of a fundamental need that all his scholarly discipline has not managed to extinguish.

    The two aspects of his nature are not at war so much as they are in uncomfortable coexistence, each pulling at him with equal force. He wants the respect that comes with academic achievement and the exhilaration that comes with risk. He wants to be taken seriously and to feel fully alive. These desires are not always compatible, and much of his life consists of attempting to bridge the gap between them.

    The Study of Evocation

    If magic is physics—and Elutherius is convinced it is—then evocation is the study of energy in its purest, most violent forms. The conversion of potential to kinetic. The manifestation of elemental force. He specializes in air and fire, drawn to their volatility, their refusal to be contained or controlled beyond the briefest moment of channeling.

    Fire, because it transforms. Because it is both creation and destruction, warmth and annihilation, dancing at the edge of control. His affinity for flame magic began early, with cantrips that sparked between his fingers, and has grown into something more refined—complex evocations that can level battlefields or reduce obstacles to ash with surgical precision. When he works with fire, he feels the kinship between himself and the element: both burning bright, both consuming what fuel they find, both dangerous when left unchecked.

    Air, because it moves. Because it cannot be grasped or held, only redirected. Wind magic appeals to the part of him that chafes against stillness, that needs motion and change and the sensation of forces in flux. He can shape gales and summon lightning, can create pockets of vacuum or walls of howling wind. More than once, he has used air magic to position himself on the battlefield—perhaps closer to the center than tactical wisdom would recommend, but close enough to feel the pulse of combat, close enough to matter.

    It's fine. I have True Strike.

    This has become something of a personal philosophy. When questioned about his tendency to stand within fireball range of the enemy, he offers this explanation with complete sincerity. The cantrip does improve his accuracy, after all. That it does nothing for his defense seems, to him, beside the point. He is there to channel the Ether into devastating effect, not to cower at the rear. Besides, there is a certain poetry to it—the evocation wizard, master of destructive force, trusting in precision rather than distance to keep him alive.

    His research focuses on the theoretical underpinnings of evocation magic: the conversion rates between Ether potential and manifest energy, the resonance patterns that make certain elements more responsive in certain conditions, the way that environmental factors influence spell efficacy. He has published several papers on the subject, all rigorously researched and carefully documented. He takes his scholarship seriously, even when nothing else about his life suggests discipline or restraint.

    The Research That Was At Least 40% Research

    There are aspects of his past that he handles carefully, turning them over in memory like shards of glass—sharp enough to cut if gripped wrong, but too significant to discard entirely. The relationship that ended badly. The succubus. The ex-girlfriend who, quite reasonably, did not accept his explanation that the encounter had been primarily academic in nature.

    In his defense—and he has constructed this defense carefully, rehearsed it until the words come smoothly—there was genuine research value in the experience. Succubi are infernal beings whose charm magic operates on principles not fully documented in the literature. Their ability to manipulate mortal desire raises questions about the intersection of enchantment and emotion, about the nature of magical compulsion versus natural attraction. He did take notes. Detailed observations on the mechanisms of charm, the physiological and psychological effects, the subtle ways that infernal magic differs from arcane enchantment. The data was valuable.

    The notes were also, if he is being honest with himself—which he tries to be, in private moments when the justifications fall away—written largely after the fact. An attempt to retroactively frame an impulsive decision as something more deliberate. He knows this. He suspects his ex-girlfriend knew it too, which is why the defense failed so spectacularly.

    The interest was at least 40% academic in nature!

    This has become his standard formulation when the subject comes up. Not a lie, exactly. Not quite the truth either. Somewhere in between, like most of his explanations for why he does things that seem, in retrospect, obviously inadvisable. The remaining 60%—the curiosity, the attraction, the simple fact that he wanted to and convinced himself the wanting was sufficient justification—those parts remain unspoken.

    He has learned from this, though perhaps not the lesson most people would hope for. He has learned that some truths are better left unexamined. That documentation can serve as both evidence and excuse. That when you make choices governed by impulse rather than reason, the best you can do is write them down carefully and hope that history, at least, will find them interesting.

    The Land That Wasn't Won

    The third land rush was supposed to change things. He had registered early, secured his ticket, spent weeks imagining the tower he would build on whatever plot the lottery assigned him. A proper wizard's tower—not ostentatious, but sufficient. A place to work undisturbed, to house his growing library, to conduct research without the constraints of university oversight. A space of his own, earned through the simple mechanism of showing up and hoping the odds favored him.

    They did not. When the raffle results were announced and his number failed to appear, the disappointment was sharp and immediate. Not devastating—he has experienced worse—but significant enough that he spent the rest of that day sitting alone in his quarters, feet propped on the desk in a position that would have horrified his posture-conscious professors, thinking about the next two to three years he would need to wait before another opportunity arose.

    In the aftermath, he has found himself unexpectedly unmoored. The tower had been a goal, something concrete to work toward. Without it, the future feels less defined. Perhaps this is for the best. Perhaps he is not yet ready for the kind of settled existence a tower represents. Perhaps he needs these next few years to determine what he actually wants, beyond the vague notion of independence and research space.

    Or perhaps the delay is simply the universe's way of forcing him to sit with uncertainty. To remain in motion rather than settling. To continue navigating the tension between his scholarly ambitions and his need for something more immediate, more visceral, than ancient texts and theoretical frameworks can provide.

    Either way, he will wait. And in the waiting, he will continue his research. Continue attending the fighting rings. Continue the slow work of becoming whoever Elutherius Aurum is supposed to be, once he finally figures that out.

    Forward, Into Uncertainty

    He does not know where this path leads. The tower is years away, if it comes at all. His research continues but offers no clear endpoint, no obvious culmination. His relationship with Sparky grows stronger, but the fighting rings are not a career, merely a passion pursued in margins of time that perhaps should be devoted to more productive endeavors. And his personal life—well. That remains complicated in ways he is still learning to navigate.

    But forward is the only direction that makes sense. Forward into the next research question, the next match, the next morning when he will wake up and choose, once again, which version of himself to perform and which to let rest. The Ether does not judge these choices. It simply responds to will, to knowledge, to the focused application of understanding. And if there is one thing Elutherius Aurum understands, it is that magic—like freedom, like identity itself—is not a destination but a practice. Something you do, repeatedly, until the doing itself becomes its own justification.

    He studies. He summons. He gambles and researches and occasionally makes decisions that seem, even to him, questionable in retrospect. He sits improperly in chairs and drinks potions that perhaps he relies on too heavily and positions himself closer to danger than wisdom would recommend, because wisdom has never been his particular virtue. What he has instead is conviction. The bone-deep certainty that the Ether is his to work with, that knowledge is worth pursuing even when the path is unclear, that freedom—messy and complicated and sometimes uncomfortable as it is—remains the highest principle worth defending.

    My magic is between me and the Ether. No gods. No masters. Just the work, and the will to do it.
